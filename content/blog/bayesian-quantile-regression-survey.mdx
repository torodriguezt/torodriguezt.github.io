Least squares estimates the conditional mean. For anthropometric data the
questions that matter are usually about the tails: which children are falling
behind, which are growing unusually fast. Those are questions about the 10th and
90th percentiles, and a mean regression does not answer them.

Quantile regression does. Under a complex survey design it needs an additional
adjustment, because the sample is not a miniature of the population.

## Informative sampling

In a complex survey, units are not selected with equal probability. When the
selection probability is related to the response, the sample is not
representative of the population, and an analysis that ignores the design is
biased regardless of sample size. This is *informative* sampling.

The common approach is to fit the model and correct afterwards. `bayesQRsurvey`
introduces the survey weights into the working likelihood, so the design is
accounted for in the posterior rather than in a subsequent adjustment.

The package covers two model families:

- **`bqr.svy()`** fits a single response by MCMC, following Nascimento and
  Gonçalves (2024). Three working likelihoods are available through `method`:
  `"ald"`, based on the asymmetric Laplace distribution, which is the default and
  the fastest, plus `"score"` and `"approximate"`, which build a design-based
  variance into the likelihood.
- **`mo.bqr.svy()`** fits a vector response by an EM algorithm, following the
  directional approach of Nascimento and Gonçalves (2026). With more than one
  response a quantile is a nested family of convex regions rather than a curve.

## Data

`Anthro` holds anthropometric measurements of 985 children, with the survey
weight in `dweight`. Age is recorded in months and converted to years.

```r title="setup.R" showLineNumbers
library("bayesQRsurvey")

data("Anthro", package = "bayesQRsurvey")

Anthro$age <- Anthro$age / 12
Anthro$sex <- factor(Anthro$sex, levels = c("1", "0"),
                     labels = c("Boys", "Girls"))
```

## Single-output models

Weight is modelled as a quadratic function of age, adjusting for sex. The
sampling weights are passed through `weights` and the levels through `quantile`.

```r title="fit-ald.R" showLineNumbers {2-4}
set.seed(50)
fit_ald <- bqr.svy(wgt ~ age + I(age^2) + sex, weights = dweight,
                   data = Anthro, quantile = c(0.1, 0.5, 0.9),
                   niter = 6000, burnin = 3000, thin = 1, verbose = FALSE)
```

The design enters as an argument rather than as a post-hoc correction.

The fitted quantiles are three separate curves, not one curve with error bands.

Each curve estimates where that percentile of the conditional distribution sits.
The distance between the outer two is the estimated spread, and it is not
constant: it reaches a minimum of 3.38 kg at 17 months and rises to 5.49 kg for
the oldest children in the sample. This is a statement about dispersion obtained
without modelling a variance.

Fitting over a grid of levels shows how each coefficient varies through the
conditional distribution.

The quadratic term decreases in magnitude monotonically, from −0.457 at τ = 0.05
to −0.091 at τ = 0.95. Growth decelerates less for the heaviest children. At the
highest level the credible interval is [−0.266, 0.082] and includes zero, so in
the upper tail the quadratic term is not distinguishable from a linear one.

The sex effect is flat across levels: −0.516, −0.559, −0.622, −0.586, −0.573,
−0.508, −0.557. Girls weigh less at every percentile by approximately the same
amount. Only one of the two covariates interacts with the quantile level.

## Estimation methods

The asymmetric Laplace likelihood is a working likelihood, not a claim that
weight is ALD-distributed. The `"score"` and `"approximate"` methods build a
design-based variance into the likelihood instead, and require longer chains.
Both are adaptive Metropolis-Hastings samplers.

At the median the two agree:

| Coefficient | ALD | 95% CI | Score | 95% CI | Width ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intercept | 6.161 | 5.813 – 6.504 | 6.113 | 5.641 – 6.579 | 1.36 |
| age | 3.545 | 3.206 – 3.909 | 3.599 | 3.121 – 4.085 | 1.37 |
| age² | −0.288 | −0.363 – −0.218 | −0.298 | −0.402 – −0.201 | 1.39 |
| sexGirls | −0.586 | −0.805 – −0.372 | −0.608 | −0.877 – −0.305 | 1.32 |

The point estimates differ by less than a third of a posterior standard
deviation. The score intervals are about a third wider, and the ratio is similar
across coefficients, so the ALD understates uncertainty by roughly a constant
factor rather than in one coefficient in particular.

Convergence diagnostics are computed for every level and stored in `$diagnosis`.
These come from the fit behind the figures above, which uses 40,000 iterations
with 20,000 discarded:[^chains]

## Multiple-output models

Weight and height are correlated, and analysing them separately discards that
structure. `mo.bqr.svy()` fits both.

```r title="fit-mo.R"
set.seed(50)
fit_mo <- mo.bqr.svy(cbind(wgt, hgt) ~ age + I(age^2) + sex,
                     weights = dweight, data = Anthro,
                     quantile = c(0.05, 0.10, 0.15),
                     n_dir = 20, max_iter = 2000, verbose = FALSE)
```

With two responses a quantile is not a curve. The directional approach takes a
set of unit directions, fits a quantile to the projection of the response onto
each one, and keeps the resulting half-plane. The quantile region is their
intersection: convex by construction, and polygonal rather than smooth because
the direction set is finite.

Individual directions are not of interest; the region is. Each region is
conditional on a covariate profile, so there is one region per profile.

<Wide>

</Wide>

The regions translate along the growth curve as age increases, and expand, since
dispersion in both responses grows with age. The contours are read from the
outside in: magnitude 0.05 traces the outermost region and each larger magnitude
a more central one.

## How the figures are built

Nothing is fitted in the browser. `scripts/extract-bqr.R` refits the three
models, extracts the coefficients, credible intervals, diagnostics, direction
vectors and the observed data, and writes them to JSON files committed with the
site. The figures read those files.

Figure 3 required one step beyond extraction. `mo.bqr.svy()` returns, per
direction, four coefficients on the design matrix plus one on the orthogonal
projection, reported as `gamma_1`. For direction $u_j$ with orthogonal
complement $\Gamma_j$ the fitted equation is

$$
u_j' y \;=\; x' \beta_j + \gamma_j \, (\Gamma_j' y),
$$

so collecting the terms in $y$ gives a hyperplane with normal
$u_j - \gamma_j \Gamma_j$ and offset $x' \beta_j$. That half-plane is the tail of
the projection, so the region is the intersection of the complements: the points
that are not extreme in any direction. Taking the other side yields an empty
intersection.

To reproduce:

```bash
Rscript scripts/extract-bqr.R
```
